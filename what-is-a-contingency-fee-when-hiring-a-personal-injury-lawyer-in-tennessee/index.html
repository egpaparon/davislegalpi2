What Is a Contingency Fee When Hiring a Personal Injury Lawyer in Tennessee

July 20, 2026 · By Shannon Davis

One of the most common reasons people who have been seriously injured in a car accident do not contact a lawyer is money.

The cost of the accident itself, while significant, is not the primary factor. The assumption that hiring an attorney requires paying an attorney upfront, before anything has been resolved, before any compensation has been received, and at a moment when medical bills are already stacking up and income may have been disrupted by an inability to work.

That assumption is understandable. It is also, in the context of personal injury law in Tennessee, almost always wrong.

The overwhelming majority of personal injury attorneys, including car accident and traumatic brain injury lawyers, work on what is called a contingency fee basis.

Understanding what that means, how it works in practice, and what questions to ask before signing a fee agreement gives injured victims the information they need to pursue legal representation without the financial uncertainty that keeps too many people from seeking the help they are entitled to.

The Basic Structure of a Contingency Fee Agreement

A contingency fee arrangement means that a personal injury attorney’s fee is contingent on the outcome of your case. If your case does not result in a recovery, you owe the attorney no fee.

The attorney’s compensation comes as a percentage of the amount recovered on your behalf. That percentage is agreed upon in writing before representation begins and is set out clearly in the fee agreement you sign when you retain the attorney.

In Tennessee personal injury cases, contingency fee percentages typically range from thirty to forty percent of the total recovery, with the specific percentage sometimes varying based on whether the case settles before litigation, during litigation, or after a trial verdict.

The practical effect of this structure is that your attorney has a direct financial interest in maximizing your recovery. The more compensation they secure for you, the greater their fee.

That alignment of interests is one of the features of contingency fee representation that most directly benefits injured clients, because it means your attorney is motivated to fight for the full value of your case rather than to settle quickly for an amount that is convenient rather than fair.

What Contingency Fees Cover and What They Do Not

The contingency fee percentage covers the attorney’s legal services, meaning the time, expertise, and professional work invested in building, negotiating, and, if necessary, litigating your case. It does not automatically cover what are called case costs or litigation expenses.

Case costs refer to the personal expenses you incur during the process of pursuing your claim. These include things like obtaining medical records, filing fees, expert witness fees, costs associated with accident reconstruction, deposition expenses, and other expenses that arise in the course of building and presenting a personal injury case.

These costs are separate from the attorney’s fee and are handled differently depending on the specific terms of your fee agreement.

In many contingency fee arrangements, the attorney advances these costs on the client’s behalf and is reimbursed from the settlement or verdict proceeds at the conclusion of the case.

The fee agreement should specify clearly whether costs are deducted from the gross recovery before the contingency fee percentage is calculated or after, as the order of those deductions affects the net amount the client ultimately receives.

Reading your fee agreement carefully and asking your attorney to walk you through the math of how fees and costs will be calculated at the time of recovery is not a sign of distrust. It is exactly the kind of informed engagement that produces a clear and comfortable working relationship from the start.

The Real Cost of Not Hiring a Lawyer

The contingency fee structure exists in part because personal injury law recognizes a practical reality: the people who most need legal representation after a serious accident are often the least financially positioned to pay for it upfront.

But understanding the contingency fee model also requires understanding what it actually costs, in dollar terms, to handle a serious personal injury claim without legal representation.

Studies of personal injury settlement outcomes consistently find that represented claimants recover significantly more compensation than unrepresented ones, even after the contingency fee is deducted from the total recovery.

The gap between what an insurance company offers an unrepresented claimant and what an experienced attorney negotiates on that same claimant’s behalf routinely exceeds the attorney’s fee by a meaningful margin.

Insurance adjusters are professionals whose job is to close claims for as little money as possible. They negotiate these cases every day. An unrepresented accident victim negotiating their claim is at a structural disadvantage that has nothing to do with their intelligence or their knowledge of the facts.

An attorney’s experience, legal knowledge, and credible threat of litigation in every negotiation cannot be replicated by an individual claimant.

What to Look for in a Contingency Fee Agreement in Tennessee

Not all contingency fee agreements are structured the same way, and the differences between them can significantly affect what you ultimately receive from your settlement or verdict.

The percentage itself matters, but so does whether that percentage changes based on the stage at which the case resolves.

Some agreements provide for a lower percentage if the case settles before a lawsuit is filed and a higher percentage if litigation becomes necessary. That structure is reasonable and common, and understanding it in advance allows you to make informed decisions about settlement offers at different stages of the process.

The treatment of case costs matters as well. An agreement that deducts costs from the gross recovery before calculating the attorney’s percentage produces a different net result for the client than one that calculates the percentage first and then deducts costs from the client’s share.

Ask your attorney to show you a concrete numerical example of how both approaches would affect your net recovery before you sign.

The scope of representation covered by the agreement, including whether it covers both settlement negotiations and trial if the case does not settle, should be clearly defined. You should know from the start what your lawyer is doing for you and when, if ever, the representation will change.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Sign

Walking into a consultation with a personal injury attorney in Tennessee knowing what questions to ask puts you in a stronger position to evaluate both the fee agreement and the attorney themselves.

Ask what percentage they charge and whether it changes based on the stage of the case. Ask how costs are handled and whether they are advanced on your behalf. Ask how costs and fees are calculated relative to each other at the time of recovery. Ask whether there are any circumstances under which you might owe fees or costs if the case does not result in a recovery.

  • Ask the attorney to walk you through a hypothetical recovery amount and show you exactly how the fee and cost calculation would work so that the final number you would receive is clear before you agree to representation.

An attorney who answers these questions clearly, patiently, and without making you feel that asking them is inappropriate is an attorney worth considering. An attorney who is not upfront about the financial terms of representation is giving you information about how they communicate before you have committed to working with them.
